          How it works

          Driver, car and era

          Every qualifying and race result since 1950 is explained as driver skill plus car, plus how well that driver suited that generation of car, plus age and experience. Teammates share a car, so the split between driver and car comes mostly from teammate comparisons chained across careers. It's fitted as one ridge regression, with penalties chosen by cross-validation.

          There are 15 generations, from 1950s front-engined cars to the 2026 active-aero hybrids. A driver who never raced a generation gets an estimate from the ones they did, weighted by how similar the cars were.

          The race

          Each race runs lap by lap: qualifying, a standing start, tyre compounds and wear (different for every car), fuel, dirty air with a circuit-specific overtaking threshold, pit strategy, rain, crashes, mechanical failures, safety cars and virtual safety cars.

          Every car races in a modern-sized performance spread, so a 1955 car and a 2023 car are compared by how dominant each was in its own field.

          How close is it?

          Real teammate pairs simulated in their own cars land within 4.7 points, on average, of how often one actually finished ahead of the other. Verstappen vs Pérez in 2023: 91% real, 86% simulated.

          Known weak spot: finishing positions can't show a huge winning margin, so very dominant cars look closer to their rivals than they were.
